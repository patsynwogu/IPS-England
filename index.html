IPS-England · v1.0 · September 2026

England issues more plans than its assessment capacity can explain, and misses more deadlines than it can explain either.

A simulation of the special educational needs identification pathway, built to test the assumption that delay is a resourcing problem. It could not reproduce England from that assumption at any level of resourcing.

Patsy Nwogu · Computational Governance
Why simulate

Because nobody records what we would need to survey

England counts children who pass through the gates of the special educational needs system. It does not count the children who never reached a gate, it does not record what provision a child actually received once a plan was written, and it does not record what happened to them afterwards.

You cannot survey what nobody writes down. What you can do is build the system as a working mechanism, give it the rules the statute actually specifies, run it, and see whether it produces the England we observe. If it does not, the assumptions inside it are wrong, and that is worth knowing.

This one did not. That is the finding.

Part one

The pathway

A child with an unmet need has to travel through five stages before the state formally recognises them. Need exists. Someone notices. A request is made. An assessment is carried out. A plan is issued.

Every stage can fail, and the failures are different in kind. A child can go unnoticed. A request can be refused. An assessment can run past its deadline. A plan can be written and still not change what happens in the classroom on Monday.

Part three

The assumption everyone makes

Ask why assessments run late and you will be told, by councils, by campaigners, by the Department and by most of the research literature, that there are not enough people to do them. Demand rose faster than capacity. The queue grew. Children wait.

It is a completely reasonable account. It is also the account this model was built to represent, because the way to test an explanation is to build it properly and see what it produces.

So: requests arrive. Each one needs a caseworker. The statutory process has a floor on how long it takes regardless of staffing, roughly fourteen weeks of sequential steps set in regulation, the six-week decision window, the six-week professional advice period, then drafting and consultation. Add queueing on top of that floor and you have the standard explanation, running.

England, in the year to January 2026. 162,702 requests for an assessment. 110,708 new plans issued. 46.1% of them within the twenty-week statutory deadline. Those three numbers are what the model has to reproduce simultaneously. (DfE, Education, health and care plans, January 2026.)

What just happened

The model traces a curve through that space, and England is not on it. Push capacity up until the volume is right and timeliness climbs to around 86%, nearly forty points better than England manages. Pull capacity down until timeliness is right, around 47%, and the model issues roughly 88,700 plans a year, about 20% fewer than England actually issued.

The two targets pull in opposite directions and the gap does not close. That is not a calibration failure that a better-tuned parameter would fix. It is a property of the explanation itself: a system whose delay comes from a shortage of caseworkers cannot also be a system that issues plans at England's rate.

If no level of resourcing reproduces what England does, then resourcing is not what is producing England's delay.

What is left is the shape of the process rather than the number of people inside it. The statutory sequence has a floor built into it that does not move when you hire. Six weeks to decide whether to assess. Six weeks in which professionals must be given to respond. Then drafting, then consultation, then issue. Those steps run one after another because the regulations say they do, and adding caseworkers runs more cases through the same fixed-length pipe rather than shortening the pipe.

Part six

The hypothesis

Assessment delay in England is generated by the mandated sequence of the statutory process rather than by assessment capacity. Increases in caseworker numbers raise throughput without materially reducing time to plan.

This is a claim, not a conclusion, and it is stated so that someone can kill it. The test does not need this model and does not need me. Find local authorities that materially increased assessment staffing, and check what happened to their twenty-week performance against their throughput. The hypothesis predicts throughput rose and timeliness did not follow. If timeliness tracked staffing, the hypothesis is wrong.

It matters because the entire reform conversation, including the implementation timetable now running to 2030, is built on the assumption the model just failed to reproduce. If delay is structural rather than financial, then more money buys more plans and roughly the same waiting, and only redesigning the sequence buys less waiting.

One extension worth naming rather than building. Appeals against refusal to assess succeed overwhelmingly in favour of families. A refusal that is almost always overturned is not screening anything. It is imposing delay, and it imposes that delay only on the families with the capacity to appeal, while removing entirely the families without it. Formalising that as a screening game that fails as screening is the obvious next step, and it is not attempted here.

Part seven

How the model was checked, and what it got wrong on the way

A model that only ever confirms the thing it was built to show is not a research instrument. The test used here is a scale audit: run the same configuration at several agent resolutions and compare the results. Anything that moves when resolution changes is describing the code, not England.

It caught two results in earlier versions, both of which were reported and then withdrawn.

ResultWhy it was withdrawn
Timeliness of 53.5% against a published 60% A discretisation artefact. Across scale factors of 400, 200, 100 and 50 the figure converged to 97%. Coarse resolution chopped authority capacity into whole cases and made issuance arrive in lumps, which inflated waiting times.
Children in high-issuing authorities 2.0× more likely to hold a plan Monte Carlo noise. At roughly 39 agents per authority the statistic is mostly sampling variation; at 158 agents per authority the ratio fell to 1.44.

The same class of error reappeared during this rebuild, in a different place, and was caught the same way. Per-authority monthly capacity was being floored to a whole number each month instead of carrying its remainder forward, which stalled queues that should have moved. Fixed before any result was taken from it.

What this version does not know. The recognition stage is calibrated to the national trend but its absolute level is an assumption rather than a measurement.
