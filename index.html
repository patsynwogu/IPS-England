IPS-England · Version 1.1 · September 2026

The Delay Is in the Regulations, Not the Resourcing

England's statutory assessment process takes around twenty-one and a half weeks to run against a twenty-week legal deadline, which is enough on its own to explain why fewer than half of plans arrive on time. Two explanations for that delay were built into a national agent-based simulation and tested against the Department for Education data. The standard explanation in the reform debate is that delays stem from staff shortages, failing to replicate England's real-world outcomes at any staffing level.

Section One

Simulating England's SEND Identification and Assessment Process

IPS-England is an agent-based model of the statutory pathway a child in England travels from an unmet special educational need to an Education, Health and Care plan. That plan is a legal document obliging a council to fund and provide the support it names, and the system producing it is known in England as SEND, for special educational needs and disabilities. The model represents individual children moving through five stages across all 152 local authorities, the councils carrying the legal duty to assess and to issue plans, each holding its own caseload and its own assessment capacity.

What is at stake in that pathway is time in a child's education. A child waiting for an assessment sits in a classroom every day without the support the law says they are owed, and the wait falls during years that do not come back. England's own regulations set twenty weeks as the limit of what is acceptable. Fewer than half of the plans arrive inside it.

The model was built to test one thing. Delay in the English system is almost universally explained by a shortage of assessment staff, and that explanation is what IPS-England was constructed to represent. The intention was to reproduce England from it and then use the working model to examine what would happen under different resourcing decisions. The model could not reproduce England from that assumption at any staffing level, and the rest of this page is what followed from that failure.

Section Two

Why Simulate

Simulation earns its place here for a reason specific to this system rather than as a general preference for modelling. The records a survey would need do not exist.

England counts children who pass through the gates of the special educational needs system. It does not count children whose needs were never noticed; it does not record what provision a child received once a plan was written, and it does not record what happened to them afterwards. Two of the five stages in the pathway below produce no national statistics at all. You cannot survey what nobody writes down.

What an agent-based model offers instead is a test of sufficiency. Rather than measuring a correlation between resourcing and delay, it asks whether a stated mechanism, run forward with individual children arriving, waiting, and being processed by authorities that differ from one another, generates the country we observe. Interaction effects that a regression treats as noise become visible in the run: queues form, clear and re-form; some authorities absorb demand while others accumulate it; Lapses appear where a rule and a resource constraint meet.

That gives a hard criterion. An explanation that cannot generate England is wrong, whatever its standing in the policy literature, and the finding below rests on that criterion rather than on a fit statistic.

Section Three

The Pathway

A child with an unmet need travels through five stages before the state formally recognises them. Need exists. Someone notices. A request is made. An assessment is carried out. A plan is issued. Every stage can fail, and the failures differ in kind. A child can go unnoticed. A request can be refused. An assessment can run past its deadline. A plan can be written and still not change what happens in the classroom on Monday.

Step through the pathway below, and two things become apparent. The first is where children are lost: roughly 52,000 requests a year do not become plans. The second is that the earliest two stages carry no national figure at all, which is the argument for simulating rather than surveying, made in numbers rather than asserted.

Section Four

What the Model Produced

Account A is the explanation the model was built to represent. Delay is a shortage of caseworkers. Requests arrive faster than they can be picked up, a queue forms, and children wait for a caseworker to become free. The statutory process itself fits comfortably inside twenty weeks, and the waiting is what breaks the deadline.

Sweeping assessment capacity across the full plausible range produced no setting that reproduces England. Tune capacity down until timeliness matches England at 45.2%, and the model issues 93,384 plans a year, 15.6% fewer than England issued. Tune the capacity up until the volume matches, and timeliness climbs to 93.2%, forty-seven points better than England manages. The two targets move in opposite directions, and the gap never closes.

Account B was built after that failure and varies in quantity. Capacity is held at a level where queues stay short, and the statutory process itself is allowed to take longer. The six-week window to decide whether to assess, the six weeks professionals are given to respond, then drafting, consultation and issue, all running one after another by regulation.

Account B reaches England almost exactly. At an average process duration of 21.5 weeks with realistic variation between cases, the model issues 108,964 plans a year with 46.0% inside the deadline. England issued 110,708 with 46.1% inside the deadline.

The model reaches England only when the assessment process takes longer than the deadline allows. It cannot reach England by making anyone wait for a caseworker.

Read plainly, the twenty-week deadline is not a target the system is failing to hit through under-resourcing. It is shorter than the process takes to run. A statutory sequence averaging around twenty-one and a half weeks will miss a twenty-week deadline roughly half the time, however many people are employed to run it, which is close to what England reports.

For a child, that distinction decides what happens next. Under the resourcing account, a late assessment is a queueing problem that more funding eventually clears. Within this environment, the wait is produced by the rules themselves, so a child whose plan arrives four weeks late is not unlucky in a stretched authority, and the same four weeks would have been lost in awell-staffedd one.

Section Five

The Hypothesis

Assessment delay in England is generated by the duration of the mandated statutory sequence rather than by assessment capacity. Increases in caseworker numbers raise throughput without materially reducing time to plan.

This is a claim rather than a conclusion, and it is written so that someone can kill it. The test needs neither this model nor its author. Identify local authorities that materially increased assessment staffing, then compare the change in their throughput against the change in their twenty-week performance. The hypothesis predicts that throughput rose and timeliness did not follow. Should timeliness have tracked staffing, the hypothesis is wrong.

A second test sits in the statute itself. The hypothesis implies the sequence cannot be executed within twenty weeks at any staffing level, so a direct audit of how long each mandated step consumes in practice would settle the question without any simulation at all.

It matters since the reform programme now running to 2030 rests on the account that the model could not make work. Where delay is structural, additional funding buys more plans, and roughly the same waiting, and only shortening or parallelising the sequence buys less waiting.

An Extension Named Rather Than Built

Appeals against local authority decisions succeed overwhelmingly. Ministry of Justice tribunal statistics for 2024/25 record around 25,000 SEND appeals registered, with 99% of decided cases going in favour of families. A refusal almost always overturned is not screening anything. It imposes delay, and it imposes that delay only on families with the capacity to appeal, while removing entirely those without it. Formalising that as a screening game that fails as screening is the obvious next step. It is not attempted here.

Section Six

Model Validation

A model that only ever confirms what it was built to show is not a research instrument. Three checks run against this one, and all three results appear below, including the uncomfortable ones.

No short-process configuration reaches England, which is why the finding is stated as a claim about process duration and not about staffing levels.

Scale Audit

The same configuration is run at several agent resolutions, and the outputs are compared. Anything that moves when resolution changes is describing the code rather than England. This test has caught real errors twice. An earlier version reported a timeliness figure that proved to be a discretisation artefact, converging to a completely different value as resolution improved, and a claimed ratio between high-issuing and low-issuing authorities that proved to be Monte Carlo noise at the agent counts then in use. Both were withdrawn, and neither appears anywhere on this page. During this rebuild, the same class of error surfaced again in a new place, where per-authority monthly capacity was floored to a whole number each month instead of carrying its remainder forward, stalling queues that should have moved. It was caught the same way and fixed before any result was taken from the model.

Reproducibility

Every number on this page is computed in Python and written to a data file that the page reads. The page recomputes nothing, so the published figures and the model cannot drift apart. The model, the source data, and the script that generates the figures are in the repository linked below, and running one command reproduces the whole set.

Section Seven

Model Limitations

Authority-level caseload and capacity dispersion are modelled as lognormal distributions rather than the published per-authority breakdown. Replacing them is the next input on the list, and until that happens no claim should be drawn from this model about variation between individual authorities.

The request-to-plan conversion of 68.0% is the ratio of plans issued to requests received within the same calendar year. Requests late in a year produce plans in the next, so the true cohort conversion differs from this figure by an unknown margin.

The variation in process duration between cases is assumed rather than measured. A published distribution of actual step durations would replace it, and the audit proposed in Section Five would produce exactly that.

The stage that brings children into the pathway is calibrated to the national trend in identification and its absolute level remains an assumption. Nothing on this page should be read as a claim about which children enter the pathway, about how many children are never identified, or about variation in identification between groups. Those questions are live, and they are not answered here.

Finally, this model addresses delay and not outcome. A plan issued within twenty weeks is recorded here as a success, and whether the provision that followed helped the child is a separate question that no national dataset currently answers.
